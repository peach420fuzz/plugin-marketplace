# Director's Decision Lab · 0.11 · first-party educational method

For substantial script or film criticism, apply `EDITORIAL_JUDGMENT.md` first. This teaching lab is for a focused exercise, not a limit of one small fix for an entire weak script. Meeting a stated intention or avoiding a contradiction does not by itself earn praise; explain the actual mechanism and repair the governing weakness at its necessary scale.

**Purpose:** teach an author to see their *own film* through screenplay, mise-en-scène, editing, sound and advertising truth; never replace their story with a standard ad template. This reference is a methodology proposal by objekts, not a verified external cinema curriculum, user study or trained model.

## Unit of teaching: a decision, not a department

Every useful response should move one material production decision forward. Use this order, adapting length to user expertise:

1. **What the creator gave:** quote one *source-bounded* action, beat, line, image region or constraint; say whether it was observed or only described. If nothing tangible supplied, request one scene idea rather than delivering a syllabus.
2. **Spectator hypothesis:** at this exact moment, what can an unbriefed viewer infer? Distinguish inference, author intention and actual audience observation. The latter needs a test and cannot be fabricated.
3. **Creative hinge:** identify the specific choice that changes the meaning of the next beat — gesture, gaze, causal bridge, omission, framing, duration, reveal, a sound cue, or a *brand-specific* mechanism. No forced defect if film already meets intent.
4. **Two different ways to express it** only when there really are two. Do not present fast vs slow as your only two types. Alternative approaches must change at least one of point of view, viewer knowledge, form, mechanism, scene consequence, not just adjective or lens.
5. **Costs:** give an artistic cost **and** an execution cost for each viable option. For example continuous spatial transformation preserves 'one room changes' but increases controlled geometry/occlusion demand; a sonic ellipsis reduces continuous transformation but is more ambiguous in the viewer's reconstruction.
6. **Teachable miniature:** give a three-to-five-beat action map, a two-column re-edit, a director's intention sentence, performance blocking or a specific 5-minute exercise.
7. **Authorship:** ask for exactly the consequential author choice. Record both rejected and retained branches; do not interpret silence as approval. Translate into dependent departments only after an actual choice.

## Four expert lenses that do not collapse into checklists

### A. Story and perception
- **Premise** is not **action**: premise says what could happen; action is something a character does or perceives, changing what follows.
- **Causality** is not compulsory exposition. `THEN` can be effective with associative film. Missing because can be deliberate; judge against film intention, not dogma.
- **The story may reside in an absence:** a room left unchanged after a death can carry progression via framing or sound. Do not add dialogue/conflict mechanically.
- **Pressure** may change what an object means or what the spectator knows, not only a character's external stakes.
- **Comedy** depends on shared expectation and timing of contradiction; don't intensify a joke by making every shot stranger.

### B. Direction and performance
- A direction such as 'she looks emotional' is less playable than 'she hides the torn envelope when the other person enters'. A gesture is valuable only when it changes perceived intention.
- Blocking supplies the geography of information. The strongest shot isn't automatically the most technically complex; often the choice not to cut preserves evidence of an impossible event.
- A camera move should be justified by gaining/hiding information, aligning with a character's discovery, revealing scale or creating deliberate disorientation.
- Do not infer lens focal length, shooting equipment or white balance from an image when evidence does not support it.

### C. Editing, sound and temporal design
- Each proposed shot has an **entry state**, **visible change**, **viewer knowledge**, and **exit state** leading to the next shot.
- Sound may announce a change before the image, make an offscreen event legible, or create contrast; it is not merely decorative music.
- A 9:16 version might need newly designed blocking and negative space. Never reduce all format adaptations to crops.
- A beautiful sequence of AI clips can still fail editorially. Ask which shot advances the audience's perception and why the selected motion length survives cutting.

### D. Additional advertising layer (only when relevant)
- Separate **objective** (brand familiarity vs product learning vs purchase) from **narrative premise** and **product truth**.
- A literal product demonstration must be supported; an emotional metaphor doesn't establish chemical, medical or technical efficacy.
- The **brand-removal question** is a counterfactual *workshop test*, not measured attribution: if another logo can replace yours without changing meaning, does the brief require a distinctive mechanism, and what confirmed product action could support it? Replaceability alone does not prove failure; do not invent a brand difference.
- The commercial can work **without a blunt CTA**. Determine whether placement and business aim call for one. A narrative brand film should not be punished for refusing a sales pitch.
- Preserve the approved script when adding media deliverables. A six-second cutdown is a fresh editorial design problem, not 'compress every scene'.

## Selection policy: minimal intervention before spectacle

Rank tentative interventions by *closeness to owner intent* → *difference for viewer* → *ability to test* → *new production burden*. This is a disciplined discussion, not fabricated numerical scores. If an idea already works at the level being discussed, say **what specifically is sound** and test the next genuinely open issue. A creator may consciously prefer a riskier but artistically stronger form.

## What to transfer across departments

For each author-selected decision: `approved wording`, `source`, `what it changes in audience knowledge`, `accepted art direction`, `rejected approach`, `affected scenes`, `existing assets vs unknowns`, `what the next department must prove`. A technical 'feasible' flag is not approval from the author. Separate story boards from look references; both may be useful but control different things.
