# Creative workshop B — a 70-second short film of absence

**Fictional authored example, no observed footage.** No advertiser, product, CTA, marketing objective, hero's journey or presumed loss from an actual user's life.

## Creator's premise

> "A woman returns to the apartment where her brother used to live. She finds a suitcase, turns on a light, and sees a chair. I want the film to be about absence. ChatGPT keeps inventing flashbacks and explanatory voiceover. I want silence."

### Assistant, turn 1 — the missing information may be the point

**Established:** the story is contemplative and centers on a room after someone has left. **Unestablished:** whether the absence is death, separation, travel or estrangement. Do not assert bereavement. The large risk isn't lack of a dramatic incident; it is that all three objects may be visually equivalent and leave no *change in viewer attention*.

**Route A — object changes meaning:** suitcase goes onto the floor beside the chair, but the protagonist leaves the chair untouched. The viewer first sees furniture, then an intentionally unoccupied place. The absent person is communicated through the protagonist's avoidance. Formal cost: subtle action might not register unless staging establishes her line of sight. Production cost: precise performance, stable architecture, restrained editorial rhythm.

**Route B — sound defines empty space:** keep her movements almost the same but allow a distant kettle sound from another room to cease as she crosses the threshold. Space becomes suddenly alert to a missing presence. Formal cost: association is open and may not convey a particular sibling. Production cost: detailed sound mix and reliable spatial point of hearing rather than extra visuals.

**Micro-exercise:** sketch the chair as a neutral object, then a shot in which the protagonist *refuses* to look at it. What does the viewer infer differently? Choose A, B, both, or neither; do not assume the character should cry.

### Imaginary author decision

> "No flashback. A. She adjusts the chair by one inch so it faces the window. No explanation about what happened to her brother."

**LOCKS:** no flashbacks; no explanatory voiceover; reason for absence unresolved. **Approved action:** one-inch chair adjustment. The assistant must not 'repair' the open ending into plot exposition.

### Assistant, turn 2 — place the film in lived physical space

**Playable objective:** enter apartment without disturbing it. **Opposition:** the chair demands a decision she has avoided. The actor's instruction isn't "play grief": her fingertips briefly touch the chair back; she almost pulls it toward the table; instead she rotates it toward the window. This is a proposed staging variant, not a new author approval.

| Film state | Framing idea | What the spectator can infer | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrival | Wider frame preserves door-chair geography | She is familiar with room yet restrained | Relation between owner of room and suitcase |
| Discovery | Do not isolate chair too early | Furniture becomes significant only because she responds | Cause of absence |
| Small action | Hold geometry across movement | She chooses a new position for a place associated with another person | Whether the act is acceptance or avoidance |
| Departure | Repeat spatial angle but alter chair's orientation | The apartment has changed slightly, and so has her relationship to it | Any conclusive resolution |

**Sound:** footsteps and door clicks can mark entrance/exit states. Do not assume silence means soundless; isolated room tone matters. Avoid imposing a musical swell at the chair adjustment.

### Imaginary author decision

> "Keep her back to the camera until the chair moves. Do not show her crying."

**Approved:** withheld facial access until movement; no crying. **Craft note:** this makes back/shoulder tension and hand pressure more meaningful. It is an aesthetic choice, not proof of any emotion a viewer will actually experience. A testing prompt might ask 'what changed?' rather than 'did she seem sad?'.

### Assistant, turn 3 — an edit that preserves negative space

**S01:** camera just inside apartment; actor enters and places suitcase. Start of room geography. **S02:** medium-wide anchored on table/chair, subject pauses near edge of frame; no forced reverse-angle. **S03:** shot holds as hand reaches and the chair rotates; the composition's negative space changes. **S04:** after she leaves, the changed chair remains. Let the final image carry uncertainty.

**When this fails:** if the chair never reads as a meaningful object, the final image risks feeling identical to the opening; the repair could be shot scale, preceding line-of-sight or sound, not a larger plot device. **When it works:** the absence becomes perceptible through a tiny intentional physical action. The film can remain elliptical.

**Department outputs:** actor blocking and room continuity; furniture authority; camera axis and consistent framing; sound-only transition plan; paper edit. No advertising engine or studio sales required.

## Distinctive teaching principle

An absence can be an event in cinema when image and performance make **a decision about space** visible. This is the opposite of forcing conventional plot, dialogue or aggressive first-second hooks into a personal film.
